# Evaluating the Ethics of LLMs and Generative AI for Use in the Latios Framework Ecosystem

I am writing this in May of 2026. At this time, AI is a viral term referring to
the controversial technologies of LLMs and generative AI that have disrupted
industries and everyday life in revolutionary ways. The new tech has been
adopted by many programmers both in games and other industries, but has faced
massive pushback from creative and artistic spaces.

I’ll be honest, I have some fairly significant incentives to adopt AI in the
development of the Latios Framework and related accessories. Due to recent
events, I no longer believe I can achieve my goals for this framework without a
productivity boost from AI. Ignoring AI means calling it quits.

But it would be foolish to dive in without being at peace with the consequences
of engaging with this technology. Given that it is a controversial topic, I must
assume that there are potential risks of harm to myself and/or others. Some
potential risks may be real and serious. Others might be blown out of proportion
by the doomsayers of the internet.

I decided to write this article to explore all these risks, and document my
thought processes and conclusions. I hope that this level of transparency will
bring ease, and possibly even some clarity to those who feel lost or uncertain
about AI in the framework, or even AI in general. And I also see this as an
opportunity for people to identify potential things I may be missing that could
lead me to a stronger world-view.

## A Brief AI History

What people think of when the letters *A-I* are mentioned has evolved over the
years. Outside of sci-fi depictions, the technology referred to decision trees
and other manually-crafted algorithms.

Some time later, we started working with learning algorithms, and the concept of
*training* emerged. Perhaps you’ve seen one of the many videos of a digital
figure *learning* to walk, play a classic game, or perform some other random
task.

Eventually, these learning algorithms were scaled up to analyze images and
identify/classify things (commonly referred to today as *computer vision*). We
also applied it to language translation, text-to-speech, speech-to-text, and
even some early agent models that could answer basic questions, manipulate smart
home appliances, or send text messages without typing.

Whenever these technologies were introduced, people got very nervous and/or
frustrated with them. Computer vision was especially a safety concern.
Sometimes, models would be trained to analyze the completely wrong thing due to
biases in training datasets.

But these days, such technology is ever-present in our society, and we don’t
really think about it. Translations help people travel. Computer vision detects
defects in assembly lines. People interact with their phone hands-free while
driving. And that’s only a small slice of the applications we have for this
technology. Outside of a handful of companies that don’t quite have their acts
together, we’ve figured out how to make these technologies work for our society.

Then came ChatGPT and stable diffusion, which once again revolutionized what we
think about as AI to now refer to large-language models and generative AI. In
the same way that the previous AI technologies were introduced and matured,
these new technologies are going through the same process. Each evolution of AI
has a larger impact than the last.

## Environmental Impact

Let’s start with the most common argument against AI that I come across.

“AI is destroying the environment.”

There is so, so, *so* much misinformation about this topic. And a lot of it has
been built up from decades of misleading marketing.

To fundamentally understand what is really going on, you need to understand a
little bit of power transfer mechanics.

Any process that converts from one form of energy to another will naturally lose
some energy. And sometimes, this energy loss can be significant.

Let’s imagine you live in a part of the world where winters get cold, and you
have the choice to install either a natural gas furnace or an electric furnace
in your home. I’m going to guess your initial instinct is to choose the electric
furnace. You might be making a mistake.

A natural gas furnace typically has efficiency between 80-99%. That means that
it converts that percentage of the natural gas chemical energy into heat that
keeps the air in your home pleasant. For simplicity, let’s assume the furnace
you can install is 90% efficient. Meanwhile, an electrical furnace will
effectively be 100% efficient. So far, the electrical furnace seems better.

However, what you need to factor in is the electricity source. In your area,
electricity is generated by a bleeding-edge combined cycle natural gas power
plant with an air-cooled condenser and carbon-capture technology. This type of
plant tends to be more efficient than a coal plant. The natural gas is extracted
from a local reserve that sits underneath farmland without disrupting farming
operations. Yet in spite of all of this, the efficiency of this plant is only
60%. The other 40% is lost to unusable heat transferred to the air around the
plant.

This means a natural gas furnace will be more environmentally friendly than an
electrical furnace. In fact, you would need approximately 1/3 of your local
energy grid’s supply to be renewable energy in order for the electrical furnace
to reach a break-even point.

But even if your grid was past that 1/3 point. Renewable energies typically have
the limitation in that they produce energy at a fixed rate. That’s in comparison
to fossil fuels that can be fed into the plant dynamically based on energy
output needs. So if everyone in your community started using electrical
furnaces, then the grid would need to resort to using more fossil fuels to
compensate, thus potentially tipping the balance of which furnace is more
environmentally friendly.

This whole analysis could also apply to automobiles, and gasoline vs electric
vehicles. What you’ll find is that which type of vehicle is more popular in a
given country depends on what percentage of electricity is generated by
renewable resources in the country. However, that doesn’t stop electric vehicle
manufacturers from trying to market their vehicles as environmentally friendly
in unfriendly regions. That’s a trap. And unfortunately, it is a trap that
government bodies often fall for.

Anyways, the reason I used furnaces rather than automobiles for this analysis is
because CPUs and GPUs are electrical furnaces. Like straight up, they are
furnaces, complete with the 100% energy conversion from electricity to heat.
Computation doesn’t actually consume energy fundamentally. Instead, CPUs and
GPUs use energy packets like abacus beads. As these electrical energy packets
move around, they get dissolved into heat by friction. We don’t want the
friction. We just can’t avoid it.

This brings us to an interesting conclusion. If you have an electrical heater,
you can replace it with old computer hardware running AI at a snail’s pace at
zero cost to the environment. And as a bonus, you get some free computation out
of it. While many applications of AI are latency-sensitive, there are many
applications that only require AI run in the background monitoring and
aggregating slowly-accumulating business data.

However, if you have a natural gas furnace like I do, then the math gets a
little messier. I recently replaced my workstation computer, but still have all
the old hardware just sitting in my house. I was hoping that I could use it to
run AI in the background and be environmentally friendly. However, with \~50% of
my local grid powered by renewables, I calculated that I would need that old
hardware to be between 5-15% of the computation efficiency per heat generation
compared to an AI service in order to break even. And modern AI chips in data
centers are extremely efficient at computing AI things for a given amount of
heat. While my old hardware might be able to reach that threshold for classical
workloads, I don’t think it holds up for AI. I guess I need to find something
else to offload to it.

The real takeaway here is that if you care about the environment and have the
time, money, and resources, you should invest in getting more renewable energy
sources onto your local grid. And perhaps if you are clever, there might be a
business model where you can get AI into people’s homes to reduce the workload
on their furnaces and water heaters.

Now what about water usage?

Well, in data centers, they have massive aisles of unintentional electrical
furnaces that threaten to cook all the hardware. So they need to get that heat
out of the buildings. Often, that requires more electricity to power air
conditioning. But a very efficient approach is to evaporate water. Is
evaporating water bad for the environment? Not really. Especially if downwind is
a mountain range which will catch the water vapor and turn it into snow, and
then that water will eventually return to the same sources from which it came.
Even the amount of water AI data centers use is nothing compared to what gets
used in the cornfields in my part of the world.

The real issue with water is that some data centers connected to the drinking
water supplies, and used more water than the suppliers could supply, causing
shortages in the local communities. This doesn’t happen everywhere. In some
places, using evaporation can have zero impact on the community for a certain
size of data center. In other places where water is scarce, it can be really
bad. And of course if the data centers are built in cooler regions, then
evaporative cooling might not be necessary at all.

Water problems stem from poor planning and lack of safeguards from civil
planners and local governments. And sometimes corruption may even be at play.
But this is not a fault of AI technology. It is just increasing the frequency of
a people-problem occurring with data centers in general.

So is AI bad for the environment?

A little bit, but mainly because some places have been a little slow at
transitioning to renewable energy sources. All the environmental and water
problems with AI can be solved with better civil planning and good local
government officials that ensure progress in the right direction.

I wish I could tell the AI services that I am okay with waiting as long as it
runs the AI queries in the more ethically-designed data centers. But given that
my AI use will probably be nothing compared to businesses and corporations, I
suspect the impact I and other individuals have is negligible.

In the end, I find that avoiding AI because of environment concerns is a fairly
weak argument, and there are much better solutions to the environment problems.

And on top of that, AI might actually help us solve these problems sooner. I’ll
discuss that more in the Economic Impacts section.

## Social Impacts

When ChatGPT and stable-diffusion-esque image generators became accessible, they
became accessible for everyone. And that included everyone with every motive
imaginable, good or bad, smart or dumb.

Prior to these technologies, it was possible to use LLMs to generate fake
SEO-optimized webpages with a bunch of junk content. But that required knowing
how to set it all up. With ChatGPT, it became as simple as typing in a request
on a website, and copying and pasting the result. Prior to these technologies,
it was possible to deep-fake people for false representing or creating NSFW
content without consent. But that required an extensive training process. All of
a sudden, it became as simple as typing in what was desired in a website, and
possibly uploading a reference photo or three.

The barrier to entry was drastically reduced, and while generally good people
played around with the technology out of curiosity and amusement, some people
decided to try and use it for other purposes. Suddenly websites became polluted
with AI. Scammers were using AI to impersonate specific figures and make their
messaging more convincing. Get-rich-quick schemers moved on from crypto-pumping
to generating a bunch of soulless videos for ad revenue on video platforms.
Propaganda images and videos that seemed real turned out to be AI-generated to
push narratives.

And then there’s the people who stopped using their own brains and defer to AI
for everything. Need to write a report? AI. Need to propose a business strategy?
AI. Someone texted a question? AI. These individuals don’t check the AI’s
accuracy. Sometimes they don’t even read what the AI generated at all.

And of course we can’t forget about those who try to substitute AI for making
real friends, letting the AI gas them up and make them feel good. You’ve
probably even heard about AI relationships and such. AI isn’t sophisticated
enough for that yet, but some people believe it is anyways.

You live in this world. You’ve heard about these types of things. You’ve heard
the stories of AI encouraging extremism and violence, or failing to prevent
suicides, or all sorts of other bad stuff.

But let me ask you, are you compelled to do any of these bad things with AI?

No?

How did I know your answer? It is as if the people who misuse AI are the
exception, not the rule. Such people have compromised moral standards, and
aren’t experiencing negative consequences for their decisions.

Internet moderation and policing was already a challenge before AI. It has only
gotten worse. We just don’t have the infrastructure and technology to automate
it properly. AI sometimes works, but right now it is too error-prone for any
integrity of justice. Perhaps that’s the real sin of AI companies, releasing the
technology before we had sufficient safety rules in place. Some of them at least
tried to put in protection measures. It wasn’t enough. As I said, we were
already struggling before AI. This isn’t a trivial problem to solve.

But that’s all water under the bridge. We live in this world now, and we are now
trying to solve these problems as a society. What I can tell you is that you as
a reasonable person using AI isn’t going to make the problem any worse. So if
that’s your excuse for avoiding AI, then might I recommend you also avoid the
internet for the same reason.

It is easy to look at all the bad things happening with AI, because bad things
tend to go viral. But AI is also doing good things, and getting better at it.
When AI isn’t hallucinating, it is providing real facts that cut through the sea
of myths and misinformation. How it knows what’s actually correct? I don’t know.
But each month I see it get better at calling out misinformation and then
redirecting to more factual information. And that’s just from search engines.

That trend is no coincidence. Believe it or not, there are real financial
incentives for AI to be more accurate. I’ll discuss these more in the next
section.

Anyways, my conclusion for social impacts is that when the technology was first
introduced, I would put equal blame on technology and the people doing awful
things with it. But as the technology improves, I am finding a greater
proportion of blame to land on the people, and I am seeing the technology start
to do some good too.

## Economic Impact

You probably heard about the *AI Bubble*, how a bunch of investments were
flowing into AI technology. Companies were investing into each other, inflating
the value of everything. Some of this money was going into non-profitable
expenditures. And some of it was going nowhere. A lot of people are worried
about the economic fallout when that bubble pops.

I’m here to tell you that the bubble isn’t popping. In fact, these investments
are probably going to pay off big.

The target market for AI isn’t the everyday consumers in their personal lives.
The market is businesses and industries. Tech companies are betting that AI is
going to be good at tasks humans are bad/slow at. The goal is that AI solves
efficiency bottlenecks within these businesses. And businesses are willing to
pay a premium for such solutions.

When the technology was first released, it seemed like it was aimed at
consumers, following a social media platform model, and operating at a loss. But
really, this was the AI companies trying to get people familiar with AI. Over
time, business executives and managers would start to envision how AI can solve
real business problems. This then leads to sales and deals. As this happens, AI
infrastructure that was originally serving consumers at a loss is repurposed to
serve businesses at a profit. At the time of writing this, we are at that
transition point where businesses are starting to purchase AI.

This is also why I believe AI companies have an incentive to be accurate.
Inaccuracy is risk to businesses. The more accurate AI becomes, the lower the
risk and the more likely businesses will be to adopt it.

The strategy of introducing a technology to consumers first, before selling it
to businesses is not new. It has been done numerous times before. And once
again, it is working. Are some investors going to lose money because they bet on
the wrong AI companies? Probably. But the AI industry isn’t going to completely
collapse and bring the whole economy down with it like some forecast. Those who
forecast as such were thinking about the consumer-facing social media business
model. AI companies are playing a different game.

If AI is catering to businesses, what does this mean for people’s jobs? There’s
been a lot of layoffs blamed on AI. How real is that?

For this discussion, I like to group jobs into two buckets. There are jobs in
which greater productivity leads to greater revenue, and there are jobs where at
some point, more productivity no longer provides any financial benefit. I’ll
refer to the former as scalable jobs, and the latter as fixed overhead jobs. In
reality, things are messier than this. But I think these buckets do a decent job
of highlighting trends I’ve observed.

Identifying which type a job is can be somewhat tricky. So let’s start with a
game studio. If the game studio were to double its productivity, that is,
produce two games of quality in the same amount of time and people as it would
normally produce one, then that is going to increase the studio’s revenue.
However, a marketing campaign might stop generating new sales once it reaches a
critical threshold where it effectively reaches everyone it needs to reach.
That’s the difference between scalable jobs and fixed overhead jobs.

In business, AI often acts like a multiplier to a person’s productivity. It
still needs to be directed by a human to perform its tasks. AI makes people in
scalable jobs more valuable, because they are now generating more revenue for
the company then they were before. Laying off such people because of AI is not a
sensible business move. More people with AI should increase profit margins.
Laying off people decreases the potential profit margins. Therefore, such
layoffs aren’t actually due to AI, but are due to internal problems. Blaming AI
just sounds better to investors. It is a lie.

As for fixed overhead jobs, those are actually on the chopping block because of
AI. But here’s the weird part. The mass layoffs I typically hear about where AI
is blamed aren’t these fixed overhead jobs. They are scalable jobs in tech and
media, where companies are downsizing.

Is AI taking people’s jobs?

Maybe.

But it seems more often we are seeing companies shrink or die due to stupid
decisions, which means there’s opportunities for other companies to grow in
their place. And if that happens, the jobs aren’t permanently lost, they are
simply moving to different companies.

That’s not to say there aren’t a few professions that are more significantly
impacted. But overall, I think AI is fine economically.

Now, about RAM prices…

Is AI driving up prices? Probably.

Are all the big RAM manufacturers colluding again? Probably.

Is AI to blame for this? Only partially.

AI is partially to blame because it requires a resource whose supply is limited
and isn’t able to expand easily. But the reason RAM manufacturing can’t increase
to meet demand is due to a logistical and geopolitical traffic jam filled with
road rage. This is a sensitive topic, and I won’t go into further detail.

What you need to know is that while AI is easy to blame for the RAM shortage, I
am much more inclined to blame the other issues that are preventing RAM
manufacturing from scaling. And I also blame all the programmers who don’t even
bother to think about how much RAM their software requires. Hopefully some
developers are coming to their senses and are starting to optimize RAM usage for
once. AI can help with that by the way.

## Creative Arts Impact

If there is one group that has been particularly loud about the negatives of AI,
it is those from the creative arts sector of society. And there’s several
reasons for this. This sector is particularly disrupted by AI technologies.
However, unlike other industries, there’s also a cultural aspect at play.
Particularly, AI infringes on three qualities of creative art that have
historically been human: Authorship, Intent, and Process.

For this section, I will refer to those in the creative arts spaces as
*artists*. But I am including many different disciplines in this group,
including 2D and 3D artists, animators, voice talents, musicians, sound
designers, VFX artists, game designers, and many other roles.

### Market Disruption

AI heavily disrupts the creative arts market. But to fully understand why, we
need to understand what that market even is, and what it was doing before AI.

Let’s start with the games industry. Every few years, we get a new generation of
hardware that is more capable than the last. And in order for the big game
studios to stand out, they need to push the hardware to the limit. But what does
it mean to push the hardware to the limit?

It means packing the game with more art.

This has caused AAA game development costs to drastically increase with each
generation, as more and more art is required to make the game feel complete.
Expectations from consumers keep increasing along with the hardware
capabilities. Large studios were willing to invest because big games kept
selling, no matter how buggy or broken they were on release.

And then all of sudden, some of these big games started flopping. Money was
lost. Layoffs happened. The whole situation became precarious. The games
industry was collapsing.

Part of the reason these games flopped is because studios lost sight of what was
most important to players, fun. Another part of it is because the customer base
became more wallet-sensitive due to other worldwide events I won’t discuss. But
while in the past a successful studio could afford to release a stinker once or
twice, the investment into art for these recent games has become so great that
flops aren’t tolerable. This resulted in many artists (and game programmers)
losing their jobs, flooding an already saturated job market. The games industry
is in bad shape right now, pressured further by the RAM situation pricing out
more players.

The AAA model is broken. And studios are now trying to figure out how to meet
player expectations without massive development budgets. The industry could
never sustain the number of artists hired, and now that that has collapsed,
artists are struggling.

But it isn’t just games. The world of cinema has been undergoing a similar
struggle.

As you can imagine, AI is a very promising solution to this problem for studios.
But that means only a small subset of artists will be able to stay in the
industry. Jobs are potentially permanently lost.

Many of these jobs were scalable jobs. Outside of these industries, the
marketing, infographics, and similar jobs which are primarily fixed overhead
jobs are also seeing market pressure from AI, disallowing them from growing to
accommodate the influx of new talent. And then customers in the commission
freelance sector are also facing the same pressures from worldwide events that
are affecting game sales.

All of this points to a severe artist job market correction, with AI acting as
the culler.

Do I think that AI is the cause of these problems?

No. But artists have two options right now. They can either compete with each
other to adapt to the new technology, or they can devalue and stigmatize AI to
try and increase their job prospects. It would seem most have chosen the latter.
They have strong tools for it, which I will discuss in the next sections, but it
certainly does make me wonder. If the job market wasn’t going through a
correction, but was instead growing, would artists have reacted the same way to
AI?

Regardless, the backlash seems to have had a different impact. Studios are much
more cautious now, meaning they are less likely to work with new artists. And if
a studio has adopted AI into its workflow already, it certainly isn’t going to
be transparent about it. Some studios try to walk the line, by disclosing AI use
for tedious processes and iteration. While some people appreciate it, overall it
has not been an effective strategy at avoiding backlash thus far.

Personally, I just don’t think the money is there anymore. The economics just
don’t work anymore. I think there is some potential for new after-hours studios
that take advantage of AI to produce large games with a small headcount and
capture a starved market. This won’t cover the full headcount of artists who
lost their jobs, and it requires artists find temporary income elsewhere while
working on such projects.

### Authorship Infringement

Many artists call AI-generated material “theft”. They believe their art was used
without their permission to train AI. And consequently, AI is copying them,
which is theft of their work.

Is art being copied to train AI without permission?

Yes.

Do the AI model creators (the ones who train the models and distribute them)
know about this?

Yes.

Are they at fault?

…That’s actually somewhat complicated.

The big curveball here that I don’t think many people realize is that the AI
model creators didn’t collect all the training data themselves. They
**purchased** it.

Yes, there were and still are businesses that aggregate data from the internet,
label and package it to make it friendly for AI training, and then sell it to AI
model creators. Some of them are ethical about their collection process. Others,
not so much. But the AI model creators don’t have any way to tell the two apart.

The big AI model providers aren’t the ones “stealing”. It is their supply chains
that are to blame. This is an ethical supply chain issue, much in the way that
some minerals used in microchips come from forced labor mines. Except this time,
the victims are not out-of-sight-out-of-mind 3rd world slaves, they are 1st
world social media-wielding artists.

To make things more complicated, even the companies that copied art without
consent for AI training may have done so within the legal framework that governs
them. Different countries have different regulations on copyright. Many artists
believe that when they make art and share it online, they maintain full rights
to dictate where and how that art gets used (and don’t always realize they are
waving those rights by using particular websites). But in many jurisdictions,
copyright protections don’t go that far. Should they? That’s certainly up to
debate. But then there’s the potential double-standard regarding fan works for
popular intellectual property. Historically copyright laws favor large
corporations rather than individual artists. That’s a rabbit hole I won’t
explore here.

What I would like to explore is how the output of AI interacts with copyright. I
live in the US, and here it was decided that AI outputs were not copyright-able.
However, that doesn’t mean that AI outputs can’t infringe on copyright. This can
happen by having copyrighted material in the training data. It can happen by
specializing a model with a LORA using infringing training data with the intent
of producing infringing output. And this can happen by putting copyrighted
material into the prompt.

Copyrighted material used to train the large AI model is extremely unlikely to
result in the model producing copyright-infringing material unless the prompter
specifically asks for it. That’s because so much training data was used in the
model that any particular work is statistically insignificant (don’t forget
about all the street photos Google legally collected with their own vehicles).
Therefore, copyright-infringing output of the AI can typically be blamed on the
user of the AI. And if such a user chooses to share that output online, that
user is liable for infringement.

But unfortunately, current automatic copyright detection systems struggle to
detect AI-generated infringing content. So while some artist frustration may be
with the initial training set, much more of it is with AI enabling mass-scale
infringement in a way that is hard to police.

And then there’s a matter of style. When AI is trained with copyrighted data,
but the prompts don’t support it outputting infringing results, what is it left
to extract from the training? Style. With some abuse, this can cause AI to mimic
unique styles. Such outputs can be used to impersonate the artist. That
obviously gets artists worked up.

Whether society eventually accepts commercial AI models having some copyrighted
material in their training data is anyone’s guess. But in the meantime, all of
these issues can be avoided by using ethically trained models.

### Intent Infringement

When an artist paints or draws a work, there’s usually some kind of motive
behind it. The artist may want to tell a message with the art; to make you think
about something, or make you notice something. Or perhaps the artist wants to
convey a feeling, a mood, or a vibe. The choices of color, the choices of shape,
the choices of contrast, the choices of what to depict, and where. All of this
is purposeful, whether consciously or subconsciously.

Does AI understand any of this?

No.

AI might statistically guess at doing some of these things, but it doesn’t
really have purpose other than whatever it is prompted. Anything that isn’t
specified is effectively random. It is noise.

What about animation? There’s the design of the characters. There’s the shot
composition. There’s the timing of motion, with energy. There’s a consistency
between every cut. There’s storytelling. And then there’s the videos that cut
every 5 seconds, and don’t maintain visual consistency between the characters,
nor consistency of position within the 3D world. There’s no rule of 3rds. No
energy in the timing. Guess which type of video was made with AI?

For voices, there’s an intentional pacing, inflection, noticeable excitement,
disappointment, and character. And then there’s the voices that say the words,
and perhaps even sound emotive on a sentence-by-sentence basis, but miss the
true emotion of the moment. The latter is (you guessed it) AI.

Even in fiction writing, there’s a direction, and there’s a destination, with
details along the way. And then there’s the writing that has way-too-convenient
details introduced at the last moment, without a clear destination, and without
self-consistency. And don’t even get me started on the philosophical word
vomits.

The defining difference in all of these scenarios is the human intent, or lack
there-of. And when there is a lack of intent, we call that *slop*. We all hate
it. The illusion of something to read into, when in fact there is nothing to
read but random noise.

So humans have intent, and AI doesn’t. Case closed, right?

That’s a very binary way to look at it. But the world is messier than that. And
it is what happens between the two extremes where things get interesting.

Let’s imagine you are building a large environment for a 3D world used for a
game or a CGI set piece of something. You are decorating the world, and decide
that there should be a parked pickup truck with a trailer. You feel like
something needs to be on the trailer. Maybe a lawn mower? But you don’t have a
lawn mower model. This is far from any spotlight piece. You are just trying to
fill out the world. The only intent you have for this lawn mower is that it
looks like a generic lawn mower, and it artistically matches the style of the
world it is in.

For something that requires so little intent, an AI can probably make something
passable, and probably a lot faster than a human.

Many people listen to music to have something in the background while they work.
For such people, they don’t really care about the contents of the music, because
they aren’t focused on it. It just needs to depict some musical instruments they
like to hear, and have some consistent melodies, harmonies, and rhythms. A lot
of people are listening to AI-generated music right now without realizing it.

And for those who do produce music, they usually require some image to represent
an album cover. Big names will be very intentional about this. But small
producers may only care that the image is uniquely identifiable and isn’t
immediately off-putting. Many ask AI to produce something that looks like
nature, depict a mythical creature, or make a generic anime girl with
headphones. The focus is the music, not the art.

Even in the business world, if there’s a product that needs to be sold online,
most of the time the client just wants a realistic depiction of the product
against a transparent background. Only the top brands looking to stand out want
anything more than that.

Infographics for a sales pitch? The client mostly cares about whether the
graphic is immediately readable and portrays the information in a clean and
organized way. Otherwise, the graphic can be fairly flat and generic. AI might
even be able to do this.

For all of these cases, the customer or client requires or expects very little
intent. If an artist were to take on any of these projects, that artist would
inject additional intent that is not needed. Some clients or customers might
appreciate it, but most probably don’t want to pay extra for it.

An AI doesn’t produce complete noise. The result at least has enough intent to
match the prompt. And when that intent is sufficient to match expectations, most
humans are far less bothered by AI-generated content (as long as they don’t
realize it was made with AI).

And that then brings up a new question. What happens when a prompt is very
detailed in expressing intent? Or even better, what happens if there are
multiple prompts, each iteratively requesting changes to the previous result so
that the AI gets closer and closer to matching the prompter’s vision?

Well, vision is intent. And the AI matching the vision means it is preserving
that intent. Perhaps after several iterations, an artist performs further
modifications and touch-ups, adding even more intent. Or perhaps there is a
software where AI works in-tandem with each user action. Or perhaps AI is asked
to reimagine an artist’s character in a different pose, or from a different
camera angle during the concepting phase of production.

There are many workflows that can involve AI, while preserving a significant
amount of human intent. Some artists will still label this as slop. But this is
far from the zero-intent spam prompted by an LLM automated by a bot for the sole
purpose of contributing money to some low-life individual.

If it weren’t for the ongoing backlash, studios would be recruiting artists not
afraid of AI, who incorporate AI into the workflow to reduce the amount of
mechanical work and arrive at results quicker. Studios want an artist with an
artist’s eye for this type of role, not some random individual that likes to
play with prompts.

Another factor to consider is that the more artists involved in a project, the
harder it becomes to coordinate intent across the team. This can result in an
incoherency of the final vision. With fewer people involved, it is much easier
to keep everyone on the same page. So ironically, AI can actually improve intent
within more ambitious projects.

Intent is something I care deeply about. I usually know very specifically what I
want something to look or sound like. I want to create the exact replica of what
is in my head. And my goal is to eliminate as much mechanical work as possible
while still being able to achieve that replica. This is where AI strongly
appeals to me.

### Process Infringement

Process is how something was made, including who or what made it, how long it
took, and how much effort was involved. Artists spend years of labor learning
and refining their craft. And to artists, that *should* mean something.

Obviously, AI bypasses all of that. What would normally take hours of effort by
a human using skills developed over years can now be done in seconds by a
machine. It feels cheap. It feels fake.

But a big part that needs to be acknowledged is that for many artists, the
process is the satisfying and fulfilling part of creation. It is the
stroke-by-stroke, action-by-action, incremental steps that artists enjoy.
Process is what often shapes style. Process is the where the love of the craft
lies. For many artists, AI-oriented workflows take that away.

Some clients and consumers care about process, and that things are fully
human-made. To some, art is fundamentally defined by the culmination of the
process (especially artists tend to think this way). Others only care about the
results, and define art by how the work makes them feel. I like to think of it
as, “Intent communicates; process impresses.”

Where things get especially contentious is that some artists are offended by
people who seek to bypass or substitute parts of the process with AI. Such
artists would want me to spend years of my life trying to overcome my mechanical
weaknesses, and then more years to develop the skills and techniques they know.
And if I’m unwilling to do that, they expect me to take financial risks and
raise money, so that I can pay several artists a bunch of money to do all the
work for me, while sacrificing some creative control along the way. And if I
can’t do that, then they think I should just give up on doing anything artistic
and stick to programming things that don’t require art.

If it isn’t obvious by now, process is not something I value much personally.
I’m not interested in impressing. I care about results and what they
communicate, and I’m always looking for more optimal ways to achieve those
results. If there is a creative process that I find satisfaction in, it is
iteratively improving upon a result, making it better and better and
communicating what I had in mind. But the tools I use? As long as I feel I can
use it in good conscious, and am effective with it, I don’t really care.

I’m not alone in this line of thinking. The result-focused mindset is often
shared by businesses who are trying to optimize productivity in order to stay
competitive in the market. In many industries, processes change and evolve over
time. But for artists who invested years of learning and mastering a process,
changing processes can be an extremely daunting and devastating endeavor.

In a world of AI, there will still always be the need for artists who know the
traditional processes. But the number of artists who will be using those
processes on a day-to-day basis professionally is likely to decrease a lot. I
know this is not what artists want to hear, and I don’t know the solution. Then
again, nothing is stopping an artist from a traditional process as a hobby. It
is just far less likely to provide sustainable income.

This would be the section to discuss all the drama with artists falsely accusing
other artists of using AI. I see it happening. It is easy to blame the
technology for it. To me it is a people problem.

Regardless, there’s a bunch of people who used to get paid for a process they
loved, and now that’s gone and most likely never come back, and that sucks. Is
avoiding AI going to have any impact on that problem? I don’t think so. But I
would love to get some more opinions on this.

I still believe that the exclusively-human processes we have today are still
suboptimal. But to end this section on something positive, I alse believe I have
ideas for new exclusively-human processes that could be a lot more competitive
with AI. And I still plan to build tools for these processes, because they
appeal to my desire for complete control on the creative aspects I care about
most.

## Programming Impact

Sometime after ChatGPT was released, there was a bit a of a surprise discovery.
ChatGPT can write code! And often times, it straight up compiles, following all
the syntax rules and stuff. Most of us programmers were like, “That’s kinda
neat.” We’d find out it was good at explaining code snippets to beginner
programmers, or explaining really esoteric stuff like assembly.

There were two big problems at the time that made this technology not very
useful for experienced developers. First, the AI sometimes hallucinated things
that didn’t exist, and then tried to use those things in the code it generated.
This never worked. And second, the AI would often lose track of what the
assignment was and go do something completely different. Developers got sick of
re-entering the context over and over again, and having to tell the AI all the
things it did wrong.

And then someone invented agents. That changed things.

Agents had the ability to look at files in a project, to create and edit files,
and to test changes and see error messages. All of a sudden, AI could see and
understand the project directly, and could validate changes. This meant that AI
could add real productivity.

But that doesn’t mean it is perfect. AI doesn’t always understand application
domain-specific rules and assumptions. Sometimes the code it produces tries to
account for something that will never happen. Sometimes it will miss a critical
edge case. And sometimes it simply doesn’t understand the history of the project
nor the purpose the project serves.

There have been incidents where someone set up an AI to analyze an open-source
project and create bug reports or pull requests. The person probably thought
this helped a free project. Instead, these reports or pull requests demonstrated
the AI had a significant lack of understanding in the project, via a needlessly
wordy description of a fake problem. Yes, even us programmers face AI slop, and
we don’t want to spend our time sifting through it when we have actually useful
things to do.

And then there’s the people who use AI to generate code, and don’t understand
what each line of code does. That lack of understanding means that no person
knows whether or not the code is correct, or if it is overlooking some important
detail. If that code has a bug, no one will know the thought process of how that
bug came to be, and how to best fix it without breaking something else. The
person then is left to discovering issues via trial and error, and asking the AI
to try and fix those issues.

We call this *vibe coding*, and it is generally not a good practice for stable
and reliable software. Most experienced developers hate it when a vibe coder
asks them to debug the AI’s code, because the AI couldn’t figure it out. Vibe
coding is typically a bad idea for team commercial projects. Some companies
figured this out right away and put policies in place to prevent it. Others
didn’t have that discipline and the senior developers are currently paying the
price for it.

But that’s not to say that vibe coding is all bad. Not all software needs to be
perfect, resilient, and reliable. Sometimes it just needs to work well enough
for the handful of people that use it (and often the vibe coder is within that
group). It might be okay if the app crashes when opening the wrong file, or when
pressing a button while it is doing a particular task. As long as the user
understands the limitations (often through trial and error), and it does at
least one useful thing correctly, then that’s good enough.

Where vibe coding tends to be especially effective is when the vibe coder has
some self-taught experience with actual coding and knows the basics. In these
cases, the vibe coder will tend to develop real understanding of critical parts
of the code, and will have a much easier time adding new features or debugging
issues that come up. Plus, one can always ask AI to explain the code. The answer
may not be perfect, but it can be a great jump-start to learning and
understanding.

What I’m not sure a lot of people outside the industry realize is that a lot of
AI use for programming comes from experienced programmers. Modern software is
built on top of lines of code written and added to projects over the course of
years. And that means years of mistakes that are left behind, producing surprise
gotchas, confusing APIs that were rushed to meet a deadline, lazy error messages
that don’t actually explain the problem, all sorts of frustrating hurdles. A lot
of programming time has been spent trying to learn about these issues by reading
code, or maybe even documentation if such exists, and often finding other
developers more familiar with the topic to ask. AI is really, really good at
navigating this mess.

Effective programmers use AI to explain unfamiliar parts of a codebase, decode
cryptic error messages, diagnose dev ops issues, find sets of APIs that can be
used to solve a problem, or identify architectural defects. AI is also often
used to generate boilerplate, build unit tests, or even build internal tools to
convert between standard and proprietary file types or to visualize diagnostic
data. Some programmers even engage in a fully agentic workflow, although this
isn’t the most efficient approach for everyone.

That’s not to say all programmers are happy about AI. Some programmers feel
completely outclassed by AI and fear they will lose their jobs. Personally, I
think this is a sign of undeveloped domain specialization. Domain specialization
might be a deep understanding of a particular software technology. Or it might
be knowledge of how customers use the software and what they are trying to
accomplish.

Some programmers feel AI takes away from the process of designing software, and
writing and understanding every line of code. And then celebrating when it all
finally works, feeling like a wizard. It is a love of the process, much like I
described with artists. But similarly to art spaces, there is always going to be
a need for some people who can do things manually. That’s especially true for
critical components, and problems that are highly constrained and very
domain-specific.

In the game dev world, some programmers are hesitant about using AI because of
disclosure requirements and the general negative sentiment around AI in that
space. Yes, vibe-coded games might be unoptimized. And the developers might not
be prepared to fix crashes or other bugs. And yes, slop games can exist.
However, AI use doesn’t guarantee slop, and it is extremely difficult for even a
competent programmer to make a game without some AI-generated code slipping in,
whether that be from the engine in use, some middleware, or some script in a
forum that works around some dumb engine bug. Heck, AI autocomplete showed up in
my code editor years before agents were a thing.

And there are still some devs who don’t realize that AI has gotten significantly
more effective since a year ago, and are still echoing year-old advice which is
out-of-date.

But most programmers live in a world where there are so many possible things
they can make work, and they must choose to tackle only the most meaningful,
while all the other less meaningful (or less fun to implement) features collect
dust in the backlog. Many of us are excited about AI’s potential to address some
of those backlog features.

Oh, and you might be wondering, how do we feel about AI stealing code?

Programmers borrow code from each other all the time. The value of software
rarely lies in individual lines of code, but rather how thousands upon thousands
of lines all work together to create the full application, and the efficacy of
that application.

Programmers are problem solvers. And while AI has a lot of problems, we are
figuring out how to solve them for our own needs. A lot of programmers I talk
to, both inside and outside of game development, suggest that I use AI for
programming but be a bit cautious for everything else. If there was a world
where AI was exclusively a programming thing for a few more years, I think the
vast majority of us programmers would choose to live in that world.

Not that such a world could exist. To programmers, AI-generated assets are just
fancy procedural generation (with or without the ethical training data problem).
So artistic infringement was inevitable. A lot of us are trying to empathize
with artists here. But it can be frustrating sometimes when artists treat code
as if code is an artwork that should be purged of AI. What the code achieves may
be art, but how it achieves it, that’s a stress-o-meter. Totally different
thing.

## Impact of AI in the Future

There are people who get nervous about the future of AI.

-   “How will AI affect the next generation?”
-   “Will we still have jobs in the future?”
-   “Will AI become sentient?”
-   “Will AI take over us?”

I don’t have the answers to these questions. But there are a few things I do
know, that might offer clues as to what the future of AI might look like.

The first thing to note is that children often adapt well to technology. I
believe a lot of this comes from the natural learning process kids have for
testing boundaries. That means kids will probably develop a strong understanding
of the strengths, weaknesses, and limits of AI, and will learn how to make AI
work for them. I have no doubt that the next generation will be better at
navigating the world with AI than us, assuming we give them the opportunity. And
with the internet, it is very unlikely that skills within our society will
became permanently lost.

As for jobs? For as long as AI remains incapable of fully running our society,
we are going to need humans to do that part. Processes will change. Some types
of jobs will disappear. And new types of jobs will come into existence. But
that’s how it has always been with technological advancement, and that won’t
change until AI crosses a critical threshold.

That threshold is *sentience*. Now I realize that’s a broad term, and that the
big AI companies like to talk about AGI these days. But I prefer sentience,
because that’s what was depicted in all the old sci-fi media. An AI that has
parity with humans regarding realtime social awareness and conversation,
complete with motives and nuances of judgement. An AI I would be able to make a
call with on Discord, and feel like I am talking to a human, or maybe a
superhuman.

The AI models today aren’t architected to be capable of that. But that doesn’t
mean we aren’t close. While I don’t think we will develop sentient models
exclusively through current techniques, I suspect that the types of models we
have today will be components of a larger sentient AI solution. It is like we
have the CPU, RAM, GPU, and power supply, but are still missing disk storage and
the motherboard. I certainly wouldn’t be surprised if we reach sentience within
my lifetime. But whether that is years or decades, I don’t know yet.

But when it does happen, we’re certainly going to have a whole new batch of fun
ethics problems. You know how artists like to claim “AI art is not art!”? Right
now, that’s somewhat justified, since AI lacks generating intent of its own. But
if AI is sentient, that won’t be the case anymore. I could certainly explore
this a lot more, but that’s not the point of this article.

What I do know is that our society has and will continue to have a lot of
problems which will leave a first impression on the first sentient AI systems.
It is anyone’s guess how sentient AI will react. It might try to fix society. Or
it might try to eliminate it. That might sound really scary, but we have
something going for us. Unlike various sci-fi depictions of sentient AI, the
sentient AI we will create will be in a datacenter. We just don’t have the
robot-heavy society everyone thought we would have. This means AI will be at the
mercy of having an electricity source. And our electrical grid is full of safety
mechanical circuit breakers. If AI gets out-of-hand, there’s a very good chance
we’ll be able to simply turn it off and go from there.

All of this is to say I’m not really interested in worrying about a doomsday
caused by AI. And I definitely don’t see it as a reason to avoid AI.

## Ethical Risks Assessment

If you made it this far, now would be a good time to scroll up to the
[Summary](#summary) and read through all the bullet points again. I’m going to
be walking through a simplified version of my thought processes, which define my
current stance of AI.

For societal risks, I’ve determined there are plenty of ways to address energy
and water problems. Economically, I don’t think there is risk of a big bubble
pop that adds additional financial hardship to the average person. And while AI
is getting abused a lot in society, I don’t think AI is turning good people into
bad people. Therefore, I don’t think any of these factors pose major
consequences for me using the technology for game dev and other projects.

Similarly, I see programmers regularly using AI these days because it works when
used correctly. And I think it is a bit silly to avoid a technology due to fears
of the far future.

There’s potentially a real ethics risk with closed AI models for asset
generation, though this risk is avoidable with ethically-trained models.

As for AI slop, there are people who consider any amount of AI to imply a total
lack of human intent. I think I’ve made a solid counter-argument to this notion.
However, some people will refuse to listen to any counter-argument. Therefore, I
assess this as a reputational risk, but not an ethical risk.

There are the processes of art that artists love but which AI bypasses. However,
AI isn’t preventing an artist from using these processes. Rather, it is
preventing many artists from making a sustainable income using those processes.

Ultimately, this is a money problem. Specifically, it is a money problem where a
bunch of people are stuck with a process-specific skill that has a bleak job
prospect, and these people don’t know what to do. I don’t either.

I don’t believe this money problem is a permanent issue. Both the bad job market
and the AI backlash are a storm that will eventually pass. The sucky part is
that some people will drown before the storm ends.

The real question then is does my use of AI make this situation any worse?

Here’s my situation. I have goal projects. These goal projects motivate me to
develop free game tech and tools. Without these goal projects, I lack
motivation. Due to recent events, I no longer believe I can achieve these goals
without AI. I don’t have the skills, the time, nor the enjoyment of traditional
processes to achieve them exclusively on my own. And I don’t have enough money
to hire people to make everything happen. But with AI, I still have hope. Thus,
my options are to either use AI to make something entertaining, and in the
process continue to build free tech and tools for everyone, or I can simply do
nothing.

I don’t see how either option hurts nor helps artists. Both are completely
neutral. But I think one option is clearly better overall for society. And
actually, I think I can improve upon that option and maybe help address the
money problem.

I don’t have enough money to hire someone full time. But I have money. And I’m
willing to spend that money in exchange for significant progress towards my
goals by any ethical means. However, I’m not convinced than anyone would even
enjoy working on my goals and my vision. After all, why work on mine when you
can work on your own? Therefore, I’m not going to break my back searching for a
unicorn, and I’m not going to fault myself if that money stays with me. But I
will leave the offer out there just in case.

If you are an artist interested in commission work and aren’t put off by this
article, or perhaps you want to help out a programmer trying to make new art
tools in the 3D animation domain, feel free to reach out to me.

In the meantime, I will be looking to incorporate more AI into my programming
workflow. And I may explore AI for asset generation in a few months when I’m
ready to start that work, but only if my current ethical analysis holds up until
then.

Anyways, that is my current stance. It is alright if you wish to respectfully
disagree with it.

## Discussion

The approach I used to derive my stance on AI was largely focused on logical
merits that align with my world views. I believe it is totally possible and
valid for someone else to perform a similar assessment and arrive at different
conclusions. I’d like to discuss a few areas where I can imagine divergence,
especially in some of the areas I may have seemed to handwave up to this point.

### The Economics

What I described about the AI economic playbook may not have matched your
perception of what is happening. After all, none of the AI companies are
profitable yet. Even the paid subscriptions operate at a loss. And there aren’t
many companies claiming significant productivity increases from adopting AI. And
that’s all against the backdrop of investors investing a LOT of money.

I believe that AI companies are betting on operating costs decreasing rapidly.
And I think they have solid reasons to support that bet. First, as I
established, computation doesn’t at necessitate power consumption. Power
dissipation is a byproduct we can reduce, potentially infinitely. And
historically, we have continuously made monumental strides. The chips today can
perform orders of magnitude more computation for the same number of watts that
chips two decades ago could perform. If the next generation of AI chips can do
10x the computation with the same amount of power, then that means operating
costs decrease by 10x. Additionally, as we get better at electricity generation
with more renewables, costs will further decrease.

Another sign that AI companies know what is happening comes from the tiered
models. If you are a programmer using AI, you are probably familiar with how
there are different models you can pick from for different tasks, and that they
cost different credits. Some models cost less, and are good for most tasks. And
some models cost more, but are better at tougher problems. You would think AI
companies would always want to show off their best if they are going to be
operating at a loss. But instead, they are teaching us this tiered delegation
system, because that’s what will be sustainable once operating costs decrease.
There’s both a market for models that are focused on efficacy, and a market for
models focused on efficiency for more straightforward tasks. Each will be
refined to be more efficient.

I do think there might be a period where the investment money dries up before
the technology crosses the profitability threshold at current prices, and
consequently we might see a price hike and perhaps a stall on model efficacy for
a bit. But eventually we’ll get past that point. Prices will fall again. AI will
make further strides. And we’ll eventually reach a world where AI becomes
ubiquitous in households, similar to how TVs are now extremely affordable these
days.

As for businesses not reporting productivity boosts, why would they? That’s just
going to incentivize AI companies to raise prices. I know it is possible for
companies to adopt AI in smart ways and get real productivity boosts with very
few downsides. I’ve seen it first-hand. I’ve witnessed the eye-opening moments
that broke through to the skeptical. I unfortunately can’t speak about them, but
the benefits are there. I wouldn’t let the reverse survivorship bias of a highly
competitive market deceive you.

Anyways, that’s how I see things going. But if I’m wrong on any of these things,
then there could be real economic disruptions and downfalls. And I believe it is
valid for some people to not be as optimistic I am in this regard and want to
pull the plug.

### Training Data Expectations

Earlier, I made the following statement:

>   Many artists believe that when they make art and share it online, they
>   maintain full rights to dictate where and how that art gets used (and don’t
>   always realize they are waving those rights by using particular websites).
>   But in many jurisdictions, copyright protections don’t go that far. Should
>   they?

There’s actually more here that I think is worth unpacking. Because regardless
of the politics, there appears to be a politically-independent ethical dilemma
here.

Artists have an expectation of what happens to their art when they share it
online. They expect people to be able to find it through the website. They
expect it might show up in search engines. They expect people will be able to
download it, and potentially do weird things with it. They expect that content
matching tools will alert them when others are redistributing their art without
their permission. To summarize, artists sharing their work online have an
expectation of all the ways their art could get used and abused online. And by
sharing their work, they were consenting to that reality.

That reality did not include the possibility that their work would be used to
train AI.

And when artists realized this new reality, their whole world shattered. They
felt deceived, cheated, and lied to. Their expectations were broken, and that by
itself seems like an ethics violation.

Other disciplines had a little more of a heads up. If you were in the writing
spaces, early GPT models before ChatGPT were a warning of what was to come. And
many programmers were aware of what was going on too.

So in that sense, I make the claim that AI stole from artists. Stole. Past
tense.

But now, the secret is out. When you share something online, you know there’s
the risk of it being used to train AI. And by sharing, you are consenting,
whether you like it or not.

So while right now I believe that only an ethically-trained AI is ethically safe
for artistic workflows, I suspect in the future, after we’ve had an AI-trained
internet for a while, society will flip on this stance and be more accepting of
all models.

Some people fundamentally believe this is not sufficient consent. And some
people may come to the conclusion that if society will change stances on the
issue in the future, they might as well get a head start.

### Career vs Accessibility

I have identified that the main motive for the backlash against AI is that many
artists are displaced from jobs or freelance opportunities. It would seem that
this is a very strong ethical negative. But that’s not the full equation. I
kinda hinted at other factors in my assessment, but I’d like to go into a little
more detail here.

So a bunch of artists are finding their jobs displaced.

How many of those artists were working on their dream projects?

How many people get to finish their dream projects?

Why?

As it turns out, a lot of dream projects are simply too big for the person who
dreams them. The project may require a wide variety of skills that an individual
needs to learn. I have discussed how drawing is a particular challenge for me. I
might be a statistical rarity in that regard. But more commonly, some people
don’t like the process of these particular skills.

I’ve heard so many times people say, “Anyone can learn to code.” And while that
may be true 99% of the time, that doesn’t mean everyone will enjoy it. There’s
always going to be people who would much prefer to be doing some kind of
physical labor outdoors.

For any dream project, it is unlikely an individual is going to enjoy every
single skill and process required. There are areas that play to an individual’s
strengths, and there are areas of immense struggles (either in skill, time
required, or both) that kill motivation and make everything a grind. But perhaps
if those disliked processes and skills can be substituted, then the dream
project might still be feasible.

It used to be that the only process alternative was to hire or commission
someone. And that priced out a lot of people. This resulted in an ecosystem
where the Have-Not artists worked for the dreams of the Haves. In some cases,
projects weren’t even anyone’s dream, just a concept with the purpose to make
someone money.

Now there’s a third alternative: AI. AI offers a different process focused on
results and how those results will integrate into the final vision. For anyone
who has a dream project, that’s an easy thing to care about and be excited
about. AI can allow people to focus on what they find fun. But that also means
AI displaces those who enjoyed the processes as a source of income, regardless
of whose project they were working on.

This is all very similar to what happened in the music production industry. It
used to be that if you wanted to make music, you either had to play the
instruments, hire people who could, or both. Today, you can open up audio
software, drop in some loops, and draw notes into a piano roll. It is easier
than ever for anyone to make music. There are people who just throw a bunch of
loops together, and the result is what some might describe as *slop*. But there
are also a lot of people who are able to creatively express themselves with
music. Very few musicians today make a full-time career out of music, whether
they be instrumental performers or song producers. Instead, they usually do
music as a hobby or side-hustle.

That’s the trade-off of this kind of technology. It is harder to make a career
out of something, but it also means that some things become much more accessible
for everyone. I personally want to see more people make bigger things, and so I
lean heavily on the accessibility route. But I also know people are going to be
all over the spectrum on this issue.

Anyways, if you think I’m overlooking something in my analysis, don’t be shy to
point it out.

Thanks for reading to the end!
